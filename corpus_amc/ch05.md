### S64
Rechargeable lithium-ion batteries offer great convenience for people in various aspects, particularly with regard to portable electronic devices, battery-power electrical vehicles and energy storage. Nevertheless, the maximum achievable specific energy of lithium-ion battery is 250 Wh kg-1, limiting their widespread application in the field that demands high energy density battery. Therefore, the most feasible approach is to seek alternative battery systems. Lithium-air battery has attracted much interest because of its high theoretical specific energy (3500 Wh kg-1 based on cathode reaction: O2+2Li++2e-→Li2O2).

### S71
An ideal cathode should possess at least three characteristics:
Catalytic activity. Catalyst is one of the main components of cathode which plays an important role in reducing overpotential in both ORR and OER process. Both homogeneous electrocatalysts such as redox mediators and heterogeneous electrocatalysts have been studied. The most common heterogeneous catalysts include noble metal catalysts, metal oxides, perovskite oxides.
Stability. It includes the stability of material itself as well as the stability towards the intermediates (e. g. superoxide, singlet O2) and final product Li2O2.
A porous structure. One of the cathode functions is to serve as the mass transport channels for O2, Li+ and discharge intermediate, e. g. LiO2, etc. Therefore, a cathode with sufficient void space is required and many different porous structure cathodes.

Challenges associated with anode and moisture permeation
Lithium metal is one the most promising and successful candidates for anodes in LABs due to its very low negative potential of -3.04 V and very high specific energy of approximately 4000 mA h/g. However, two key issues still must be overcome to develop a suitable lithium metal anode for LABs. The first problem is the lithium dendrite growth, which causes internal short circuiting of LABs during charge/discharge cycles. Moreover, the second problem is the lower efficiency of charge/discharge; many reasons contribute to its occurrence, such as dead lithium synthesis that is irreversible and the incessant growth of the SEI layer.

### S81
The penetration of unwanted moisture is also a crucial challenge. Particularly, lithium must be isolated from moisture permeation to the anode area due to its high reactivity. Considering that most researchers prefer a highly pure oxygen environment for LAB research, it is difficult to completely exclude the water from the air electrode. There is also a high probability of electrolyte contamination with water in long operations.Even trace amounts of water can cause rapid corrosion of the lithium metal anode, which leads to poor performance of LABs and serious safety issues. Hence, anode stability with electrolytes containing trace water is the most crucial aspect for a long operation of LABs.

### S86
Instead of intercalation, lithium–sulfur batteries operate based on metal plating and stripping on the lithium anode side and a conversion reaction on the sulfur cathode side. The non-topotactic nature of these reactions endows lithium anodes and sulfur cathodes with high theoretical specific capacities of 3860 and 1673 mA h g-1 respectively. Together with an average cell voltage of 2.15 V, this gives lithium–sulfur batteries a high theoretical energy density of ~2500 W h kg-1 or 2800 W h L-1. Moreover, sulfur is cheap and readily abundant in the Earth’s crust, which makes lithium–sulfur batteries a particularly attractive and low-cost energy storage technology.

On the sulfur cathode side, these challenges include:
Dissolution of intermediate lithium polysulfides into the electrolyte.
During the cycling process, intermediate long-chain lithium polysulfide species (Li2S4 to Li2S8) dissolve readily into ether-based electrolytes. This leads to continuous loss of active material into the electrolyte, some of which remain dissolved and do not precipitate back onto the cathode as lithium sulfide at the end of discharge. As a result, low discharge capacities and rapid capacity decay are often observed during cycling.

### S91
The challenges for the lithium–sulfur batteries
On the lithium anode side, these challenges include:
Polysulfide shuttle effect.
Long-chain lithium polysulfides (Li2S4 to Li2S8) which dissolve into the electrolyte can diffuse to the lithium anode to be reduced chemically (instead of electrochemically) to form lower-order polysulfides, which can then diffuse back to the sulfur cathode to be re-oxidized. This parasitic polysulfide shuttle effect, which is essentially an internal short, leads to self-discharging and low Coulombic efficiency during cycling.

The challenges for the lithium–sulfur batteries
(b) Non-uniform solid electrolyte interphase (SEI).
Lithium metal is highly reactive and reacts with the electrolyte to form
an SEI on the surface, which is ionically-conducting but electronically-insulating. However, in most cases, the SEI is non-uniform and does not passivate the lithium metal surface sufficiently, leading to continuous, undesirable side reactions
with the electrolyte. This consumes both lithium metal and electrolyte, resulting in poor reversibility and low Coulombic efficiency during repeated plating and stripping.

### S94
The challenges for the lithium–sulfur batteries
(c) Dendrite growth of lithium metal.
The growth of lithium dendrites leads to continuous breaking and reforming of the SEI, which further consumes lithium metal and electrolyte. This causes the battery to eventually fail due to depletion of electrolyte and high impedance through the thick SEI. Dendrites that break off will lost contact with the lithium metal anodes and become dead lithium, resulting in further loss of Coulombic efficiency. Moreover, dendrites can potentially penetrate the separator and lead to internal short circuits, posing serious safety hazards.

### S104
Introduction
Li metal is regarded as an ideal option for use as a battery anode for the future due to the highest theoretical capacity (3860 mA h g-1) and the lowest electrochemical potential (-3.04 V vs. the standard hydrogen electrode) among all the possible candidates.
A battery with a Li anode exhibits a higher energy density:
LIBs with graphite anodes and lithium transition metal oxide (LMO) cathodes can achieve a specific energy of 250 W h kg-1, Li-LMO batteries would deliver a specific energy of 440 W h kg-1 if the anode is replaced with metallic Li.
The realistic specific energy of Li–S batteries is 600 W h kg-1, which is twice that of LIBs.
Li–air batteries, whose realistic specific energy may reach up to 950 W h kg-1, are regarded as an ideal battery system.
